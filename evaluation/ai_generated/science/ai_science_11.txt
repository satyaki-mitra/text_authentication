Phenomenon: Aurora Borealis (Northern Lights) and Aurora Australis (Southern Lights)

Explanation: The Northern and Southern Lights, also known as auroras, are stunning natural light displays that occur in polar regions. These celestial spectacles are caused by the interaction between solar particles and Earth's magnetic field.

1. Solar activity: The sun continually emits charged particles, primarily electrons and protons, into space. These charged particles form the solar wind, which streams away from the sun at approximately 400-750 kilometers per second (around 800-1600 miles per second).

2. Earth's magnetic field: The Earth has a magnetic field generated by its molten iron core. This magnetic field acts as a protective shield, deflecting the majority of solar particles away from the planet. However, near the poles, the Earth's magnetic field lines converge, allowing some charged particles to be funneled towards the polar regions.

3. Collision with atoms in Earth's atmosphere: As the charged particles approach Earth, they collide with gas molecules in the upper atmosphere, about 80-640 kilometers (50-400 miles) above the surface. This collision excites and ionizes the gas molecules, causing them to emit light.

4. Light emission: The specific colors of the aurora depend on the type of gas molecule involved in the collision and the energy level of the charged particles. Oxygen typically emits green and red lights, while nitrogen produces blue and purple hues. The swirling patterns and shifting colors of the auroras are due to the movement of charged particles and magnetic fields in Earth's atmosphere.

5. Aurora visibility: To observe the Northern Lights, one must be located within the Arctic Circle, while Southern Lights can be seen in Antarctica or other regions south of the Antarctic Circle. The best times for viewing auroras are during periods of increased solar activity (solar maximum) and clear, dark nights with minimal light pollution.
