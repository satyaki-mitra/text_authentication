Phenomenon: Aurora Borealis (Northern Lights) and Aurora Australis (Southern Lights)

Explanation:

The aurorae, also known as the Northern Lights in the northern polar regions and Southern Lights in the southern polar regions, are natural light displays that occur due to the interaction between solar particles and Earth's magnetic field.

1. Solar activity: The sun constantly emits a stream of charged particles, including electrons and protons, known as the solar wind. These particles travel through space at high speeds and can reach Earth's magnetosphere (the region around our planet where its magnetic field dominates).

2. Earth's magnetic field: Our planet has an intrinsic magnetic field that is generated by its molten iron core. This magnetic field acts as a shield, deflecting most of the solar wind away from Earth. However, some of these charged particles become trapped in the magnetosphere.

3. Magnetic field lines: The Earth's magnetic field lines are not straight but form an arc-like shape around the planet, with the north and south poles having open magnetic field lines that extend out into space. These open field lines create a sort of "funnel" for the solar particles to travel along.

4. Energy transfer: As the charged particles move along Earth's magnetic field lines, they get funneled towards the polar regions, where the magnetic field lines converge and reconnect at altitudes of approximately 100-400 kilometers (62-249 miles). When these particles collide with molecules in the Earth's upper atmosphere (thermosphere and ionosphere), they transfer their energy to these atoms and molecules, causing them to become excited and emit light.

5. Light emission: The energy levels of excited gas molecules can change through a process called relaxation, during which they release energy in the form of photons – tiny packets of light. Different gases within Earth's atmosphere (such as oxygen, nitrogen, and rare noble gases) produce light at specific wavelengths when they are excited. In the case of the aurorae, the dominant colors observed are green, red, blue, and violet, which correspond to different energy levels of these gas molecules.
