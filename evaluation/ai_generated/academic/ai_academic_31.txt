Title: An Examination of the Interplay between Cognitive Flexibility, Working Memory, and Executive Functions in Adolescent Reading Comprehension

Abstract:

This study aims to delve into the intricate relationship between cognitive flexibility, working memory, and executive functions as they pertain to adolescent reading comprehension. The investigation is grounded in the theory that these three interrelated constructs play a pivotal role in the effective processing of written language during the critical developmental stage of adolescence.

The study employed a mixed-methods design, combining standardized psychometric assessments and qualitative analyses of self-reported reading experiences. A sample of 200 adolescent participants, aged 13-18 years, was recruited from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds to ensure a broad representation of the population under investigation.

The primary outcome measures included tests of cognitive flexibility (e.g., Trail Making Test), working memory (e.g., Digit Span Test), and executive functions (e.g., Stroop Test). These measures were administered prior to and following a reading comprehension task, enabling an analysis of any changes in cognitive performance before and after the reading exercise.

Qualitative data were gathered through semi-structured interviews, where participants were asked to discuss their experiences during the reading comprehension task, focusing on strategies employed, challenges encountered, and self-perceived improvements in cognitive abilities following the task. The interviews were transcribed verbatim and coded using thematic analysis techniques to identify recurring themes and patterns.

Preliminary findings suggest that there is a significant correlation between cognitive flexibility, working memory, and executive functions, and adolescent reading comprehension. Specifically, higher levels of cognitive flexibility, working memory capacity, and effective executive function management are associated with improved reading comprehension outcomes. Furthermore, qualitative data revealed that participants who demonstrated stronger cognitive abilities reported utilizing more sophisticated reading strategies and experiencing fewer difficulties during the task.

The study's findings have substantial implications for educational practices and interventions aimed at enhancing adolescent reading comprehension. The results underscore the importance of addressing cognitive flexibility, working memory, and executive functions in concert when developing strategies to support struggling readers or those with learning disabilities. Moreover, the study provides insights into the developmental trajectory of these critical skills during adolescence, offering opportunities for targeted interventions tailored to specific age groups.
