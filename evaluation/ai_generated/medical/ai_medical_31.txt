Title: A Rare Case of Prolonged Hypokalemia in a Pediatric Patient with Cystic Fibrosis

Background: Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a genetic disorder characterized by persistent lung infection, inflammation, and obstructive airway disease. While hypokalemia, or low potassium levels, is not uncommon in CF patients due to various factors such as medication use, sweating, and gastrointestinal losses, this case report highlights an unusual instance of prolonged hypokalemia in a pediatric patient with CF.

Case Presentation: A 12-year-old male patient with a known diagnosis of CF presented to the emergency department (ED) with recurrent vomiting and lethargy over the past three days. His medical history included pancreatic insufficiency, frequent respiratory infections, and chronic use of inhaled bronchodilators and hypertonic saline.

On admission, his vital signs were stable, but laboratory results revealed a serum potassium level of 2.1 mmol/L (normal range: 3.5-5.0 mmol/L). The patient was not taking any medications known to cause hypokalemia and had no history of diuretic use. He also denied excessive sweating or recent changes in diet.

In the ED, the patient was started on potassium supplements intravenously. His electrolytes were closely monitored, and he was discharged after his serum potassium level normalized. However, within a week, the patient presented again with similar symptoms of hypokalemia, despite adherence to the prescribed treatment regimen.

Further investigation revealed that the patient had developed a new, persistent cough associated with increased sputum production and worsening respiratory function tests. Chest X-ray showed bilateral infiltrates, suggesting bacterial superinfection. The patient was treated with antibiotics and his potassium levels were closely monitored.

Over the subsequent months, the patient experienced multiple episodes of hypokalemia, each requiring hospitalization and treatment.
