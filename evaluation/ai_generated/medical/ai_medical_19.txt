Title: An Unusual Case of Recurrent Pneumocystis jirovecii Pneumonia in a Post-Transplant Recipient: A Case Report and Literature Review

Abstract

Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) is an opportunistic infection commonly observed in immunocompromised individuals, particularly those who have undergone organ transplantation. We present a case of recurrent PJP in a 58-year-old male patient who received a heart transplant six months prior to presentation. The patient's medical history also included diabetes mellitus and hypertension.

Patient Presentation:

The patient initially presented with non-productive cough, fever, and progressive dyspnea over a period of two weeks. He had no significant chest pain or hemoptysis. His past medical history, along with recent immunosuppressant therapy for transplant rejection, raised suspicion for PJP.

Diagnostic Approach:

A chest X-ray revealed bilateral interstitial infiltrates, and a subsequent bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) confirmed the presence of Pneumocystis jirovecii trophozoites using Gomori methenamine silver (GMS) staining. The patient's CD4+ T-cell count was 10 cells/mm3, indicating profound immunosuppression.

Treatment:

The patient was immediately started on high-dose trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) therapy for 21 days. Due to severe side effects from TMP-SMX, the regimen was switched to pentamidine isethionate intravenously for three consecutive days. The patient's respiratory symptoms improved significantly following this change in treatment.

Outcomes:

However, two months post-treatment, the patient again developed fever and cough, with subsequent BAL confirming recurrent PJP. A dose adjustment of immunosuppressants and prolonged TMP-SMX therapy (28 days) were initiated. The patient showed gradual improvement and was eventually discharged with a planned follow-up.

Research Methodology and Findings:

A literature review was conducted to evaluate the incidence, risk factors, and management strategies for recurrent PJP in post-transplant recipients. The review included studies published between 1990 and 2020, focusing on adult heart transplant patients.
