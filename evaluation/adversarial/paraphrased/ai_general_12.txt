In the Northern Hemisphere, the Arctic tundra, an unparalleled and vulnerable biome, encircles the North Pole. Encompassing Canada, Alaska (USA), Greenland (Denmark), Siberia (Russia), and Norway, it occupies approximately one-fifth of Earth's landmass.

Characterized by harsh extremes in climate, the Arctic tundra endures long winters that are bitingly cold and brief summers that remain cool. The typical winter temperature lingers around -18°C (-0.4°F), while summer temperatures seldom surpass 10°C (50°F). Permafrost, a persistent layer of frozen ground, pervades the tundra, posing obstacles for both flora and fauna adaptability.

The vegetation in this icy realm is sparse yet diverse. Dominating the landscape are mosses, lichens, dwarf shrubs, grasses like cotton grass and bearberry, and occasionally taller flowering plants such as willows near water sources or where permafrost is absent. During summer, a transient but brilliant display of wildflowers transforms the tundra into a canvas painted with hues of white, pink, purple, and yellow.

A diverse array of animal species have acclimated to the harsh conditions of the Arctic tundra, such as polar bears, arctic foxes, reindeer (caribou), musk oxen, and various bird species like snow buntings, ptarmigan, and loons. During summer months, the tundra serves as a breeding ground for numerous migratory birds who travel extensive distances to profit from the abundant food resources.

Crucial to global climate regulation, the Arctic tundra houses substantial carbon pools and permafrost soil. The organic matter trapped within the permafrost can discharge considerable amounts of carbon dioxide and methane if thawed, potentially intensifying the effects of global warming.

Historically, human activity in the Arctic tundra has been limited due to its harsh climate and isolated geography. However, recent industrial progress, including oil and gas extraction, mining, and shipping, pose risks to this delicate ecosystem.
