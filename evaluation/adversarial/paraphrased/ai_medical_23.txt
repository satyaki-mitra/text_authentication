Title: An Unusual Incident of Acute Intermittent Porphyria: A Test for Diagnosis and Treatment Strategy

Summary

Acute Intermittent Porphyria (AIP), a seldom, hereditary metabolic disease marked by neurovisceral attacks, is the focus of this case study. This report underscores the diagnostic conundrums and therapeutic strategies employed in the treatment of a 45-year-old woman who exhibited symptoms suggestive of an acute abdomen, ultimately leading to a delayed diagnosis of AIP.

Patient Profile:

The patient, a 45-year-old Caucasian female, sought medical attention in the emergency department, enduring severe abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, constipation, and peripheral neuropathy for three days. The patient's medical history was devoid of similar occurrences; however, she possessed a family history of enigmatic maladies and premature demises. Upon examination, her temperature was normal, but she exhibited tachycardia (120 beats per minute) and hypertension (180/100 millimeters of mercury). A physical abdominal assessment revealed generalized tenderness and guarding without rebound or rigidity. Preliminary lab tests demonstrated leukocytosis (white blood cell count: 15.6 x 10^3/μL), elevated C-reactive protein (12 milligrams per liter), and normocytic anemia (hemoglobin: 9 grams per deciliter). An abdominal CT scan suggested appendicitis due to multiple thickenings in the appendiceal wall, prompting a laparoscopic appendectomy. However, post-surgical recovery was marred by persistent abdominal pain, recurrent vomiting, and deteriorating neuropathy.
