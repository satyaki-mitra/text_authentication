Title: The Enigma of Supercooling and the Genesis of Hailstones

Unraveling a Unique Thermodynamic Phenomenon

Supercooling, an anomalous thermodynamic process, occurs when a liquid like water persists in its fluid state below its freezing point without crystallizing. This peculiarity significantly contributes to the genesis of hailstones, a captivating natural weather event that has puzzled scientists for ages.

Mechanisms Behind Supercooling and Hailstone Formation

Supercooled water droplets in clouds, cooled below their freezing point (0°C or 273.15K), escape crystallization due to the suppression of heterogeneous nucleation. This process necessitates foreign particles such as dust or ice crystals for water molecules to bond and form ice crystals. In cloud settings, the scarcity of these nuclei allows supercooled water droplets to persist.

However, when these supercooled droplets collide with other large supercooled droplets or small ice crystals (homogeneous nucleation), they initiate a process known as the Bergeron-Findeisen process. This process entails the conversion of supercooled water droplets into ice crystals, releasing heat and increasing in size. These newly formed ice crystals are called embryonic hailstones.

As embryonic hailstones grow, they attract more supercooled water droplets through a process termed riming. The added water freezes on the surface of the hailstone and facilitates its growth. Moreover, the upward motion of the hailstone within the cloud causes melting at warmer temperatures, followed by refreezing as the hailstone ascends to colder regions, thereby fostering its expansion. This repeated sequence of riming and melting-refreezing results in the formation of a robust, spherical hailstone.

Empirical Evidence

Various experiments have been executed to comprehend the mechanics of supercooling and hailstone formation.
