Title: Investigating the Effects of Climate Alteration on Phytoplankton Biodiversity in the North Pacific Ocean: A Long-term Examination

Summary

Background: The North Pacific Ocean, a critical biogeochemical hub, has undergone substantial climate modifications over the last few decades. These shifts in environmental factors may have influenced the richness and distribution of phytoplankton, crucial primary producers that sustain marine food chains. Acknowledging these alterations is vital for predicting future ecological patterns and managing marine resources responsibly.

Research Approach: We undertook a long-term examination (1950-2018) employing satellite ocean color data to scrutinize the temporal and spatial variations in phytoplankton biodiversity within the North Pacific Ocean. The study applied the Modified Shannon Index as a measure of phytoplankton diversity, while factors such as sea surface temperature (SST), chlorophyll-a concentration (Chl-a), and primary productivity were derived from the same dataset for correlation analysis. A generalized additive model (GAM) was utilized to evaluate the relationship between climate change markers (SST and Chl-a) and phytoplankton diversity.

Main Conclusions: Our findings demonstrated a substantial decrease in phytoplankton biodiversity throughout the study period, with a more pronounced dip observed post-1980s. The GAM analysis suggested that both SST and Chl-a were positively correlated with phytoplankton diversity, implying that rising temperatures and increased nutrient availability could boost diversity temporarily but might result in detrimental impacts in the long term due to eutrophication and alterations in species makeup.

Consequences: The detected decline in phytoplankton biodiversity may have repercussions for marine food webs, fisheries productivity, and carbon cycling.
